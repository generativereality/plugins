# Copy and story: what to say first, and how it holds attention

This file covers how words and stories are built for any piece people read, watch or hear. Read
`voice.md` beside it: this file decides what to say, and that one decides how the sentences sound.

Sources: *Made to Stick* (Heath & Heath), *Breakthrough Advertising* (Schwartz), Ogilvy, Copyhackers
(Wiebe), *Everybody Writes* (Handley), *Building a StoryBrand* (Miller), *$100M Offers* and *$100M
Leads* (Hormozi), *The Wizard of Ads* (Williams), *Influence* (Cialdini), and about 120 advice videos
from working creators and ad buyers, transcribed and read frame by frame (Nov 2026). The creators'
numbers are their own, often from people selling courses: use them as a creator's benchmark, never as a
fact in a claim. Where a book and a video disagree on tone, follow the book.

## The failure this file exists to prevent

A piece built from what the writer can cite instead of what the reader cares about: the product
introduced, then counts, then features. Every line is true and a stranger stops after the first one.
That is the *curse of knowledge*: once you know a thing, you can't see it as someone who doesn't.
**Evidence backs the copy. It isn't the copy.**

## 1. The one idea

Before a line of copy, write the core as one sentence that still holds when everything else is cut.
Then make it stick:

- **Simple:** one core, short and deep. Not a list of everything.
- **Unexpected:** break the reader's pattern, open a gap in what they know, then fill it. The surprise
  serves the core or it is a gimmick.
- **Concrete:** something a person does or sees. *"It found the three people who already recommend you"*,
  not *"relationship intelligence"*.
- **Credible:** proof the reader can test: a demo, a free try, a real output.
- **Emotional:** one person beats a statistic. In the book's study, a letter about one named child
  raised more than twice what a letter of statistics did, and adding the statistics lowered it.
- **A story:** a short sequence of what happened to someone, which the reader can run in their head.

Write three to five angles for the idea, pick the one a stranger in that channel would stop for, and
say in one line why it beat the others.

## 2. Study what already works {#study}

In the niche and the channel, find the pieces that did **3 to 5 times** better than their own
author's normal (by views, saves or upvotes against that author's median). Copy their hook **form**
and structure, never their content. Then fill the form with what only this person can say: *what have
they earned the right to explain?* Keep a bank of the audience's problems in their exact words, from
comments, threads and reviews of the alternatives. Their phrases make the best headlines. Never
invent a channel, a link or a number while studying. Write *"not verified"*.

## 3. Hooks

**The first one or two sentences (or seconds) decide everything, and clarity beats cleverness.** Say
what this is about and why it matters to this reader, in short plain words. A great hook isn't
catchy; it is a trap the reader walks into because it is about them.

Formulas, one per piece:

- **Context, pull, turn:** line one says exactly what this is; then a pull (something hidden that is
  happening to them, a contradiction, or proof shown); then the opposite of what they expect.
- **Context, challenge, transition:** *"…so today is day one of… But let me start from the
  beginning."* It opens a series.
- **Connect to what they already want:** *"a dinner that gets you a third date"*, not *"knife skills"*.
- **Audience qualifier:** *"If you sell anything, you need to hear this."*
- **I built X because Y**, and **the problem, then what I did about it**. These are the commonest
  shape of top maker posts.
- **Confession:** a true, specific thing that went wrong.
- **Receipts first:** a real, small, verifiable number in the first line or first second. Only true
  ones.
- **Newsjack:** this week's change, ruling or announcement.
- **Attention triggers:** an extreme size or speed, a specific large number, a person the reader
  wants to become, a big result from no experience, a line going up and to the right.

**Visual hooks** (the first one or two seconds of a video are a picture): a slow reveal of the result,
before and after, a list that unblurs item by item, a tier board, a split screen, a prop, a walk-in, a
pinned title bar with the claim, a counter that climbs. A kept imperfection (a dog walking through)
works as a pattern interrupt. The first visual must match the first words.

**Text hooks:** LinkedIn rewards a hook that gets to its purpose at once; X rewards a plain contrarian
line or a prediction. *"Press release energy"* fails everywhere. Never open on the product's name for a
reader who hasn't heard of it.

## 4. Scripts and structure

- **Stories are loops:** stakes, an open question, an unexpected turn, a re-hook, then again. Answering
  one question opens the next. Every loop closes; pay the promise before 80% of the runtime; never
  summarise at the end.
- **Name the framework early.** A video that took 22 seconds to say what it was about flopped.
- **Entertain the many, teach the interested, sell to the committed.** If it looks like an ad, people
  scroll.
- **Tell the story while doing something visually interesting** (cooking, walking, building).
- **Show, don't list.** Lists with no opinion stopped working. Showing the expertise (a case study, a
  worked hypothetical, a live build, the product doing the thing) is where the room is.
- **Structures that work:** man in a hole (fine, worse, better); big goal, challenge, breakthrough;
  before, bridge, after. Use one per piece, filled with the customer's words and invisible to the reader.
- **For a 30 to 60 s ad or a song-led piece:** shock and an open loop; personal stakes; failed fixes and
  a low point; a guide and a new explanation; doubt and a demonstration; progress you can follow; the
  payoff and who they are now; the reveal; one invitation that calls back to the start. The test is
  whether a first-time viewer could retell what changed.
- **Carousels and list posts:** each item carries its proof and an opinion; a list that overflows the
  screen gets saved.
- **Long video:** a counterintuitive opening, a promise of value, then deliver it at once.
- **Newsletters:** frameworks and breakdowns, no filler. It is the highest-intent channel there is.

## 5. Copy rules

- **The headline does most of the work.** It promises something the reader wants, and is specific.
- **Feel first, then ask.** Let the reader see their own situation in concrete words before any ask.
- **Specifics over adjectives:** swap every adjective for a fact, a named situation or a visible
  result. A slogan and vague adjectives insult the reader.
- **The reader's own words** (§2) write the headline and set the order.
- **Write to one person.** Draft fast, then cut everything that is there for the writer.
- **The reader is the hero; the product or person is the guide** with a plan and one next step.
- **Value is outcome × likelihood ÷ (time × effort).** Showing how soon the first result comes and how
  little it asks usually beats a bigger promise, which lowers belief with sceptical readers.
- **Proof, honestly:** social proof works best from people like the reader, and only when true and
  specific (named only with consent). Give first: the piece itself should be useful. Use scarcity and
  authority only when they are real.
- **Plan what the reader sees with what they read.** For an article, page, guide or launch, plan the
  picture in the brief (the real screen, a diagram, the chart of the number that matters, a before and
  after) and write around it. One visual per idea it explains, with a caption saying what to notice.
- **A landing page is one promise and one button,** and the visitor feels the win in under a minute.
  For a launch that has to explain *why*: the insight (what changed, or what the reader gets wrong), the
  alternatives and their cost, the better way, then the product, its proof, and one ask. Most pages
  are one sentence too long.
- **The call to action** says what to do, why, when, how easy it is and what happens next. It can be
  the lesson itself. Never ask for upvotes, shares or support, and always say in the first line that
  you made the thing.

## 6. What people share

One specific relationship, one small concrete moment, a reveal or a line that lands at the end, and an
implicit *"send this to…"*. Content made to be sent to one person is what gets passed on. People trust
"people like me" over the best success story, and a moment they can picture over a number (*"close the
laptop at five"*, not *"save five hours a week"*). Raw beats produced: smooth AI transitions and
sharpening push a piece toward looking like an ad. Less production often performs better:
platform-native, phone-shot, talking video beats a polished studio piece.

## 7. Formats and series

- **A series beats one-offs.** A named recurring format (*"day 1 of…"*, *"one teardown a week"*) makes
  the audience a participant. A **default piece** that is easy to make every week keeps the cadence.
- **Kinds to rotate:** teaching, story, authority (the "if I started again today" case study), series,
  and doubling down on a hit by turning it into a format.
- **Pieces every profile needs:** the origin story, how it works, how it was made, a day in the life,
  the before and after (the maker's and a customer's), the mission, a hypothetical.
- **Formats that work as ads and as posts:** prototype to product, blind test, stress test, notes-app
  screenshot, "don't buy this if…", teardown, reply to a real comment, day 1 to day 30, price
  breakdown, screen recording, founder demo, raw review.
- **One core piece, cut for each channel** (post, short clip, carousel, newsletter section, a reply in
  the thread where someone asked exactly that). Use one home platform for the original work and treat
  the rest as distribution, in the same voice.
- **Own a category, a word and a visual,** and repeat them past the point of boredom.
- **Old drafts and archived posts are material.** Refresh and republish what still holds before
  inventing a new topic, and mine the person's voice notes for ideas (a memo saying "idea for a post"
  is a brief waiting to be written).

## 8. Ads

Distinct creative is the targeting now. Ad delivery systems reward distinct concepts and count
near-duplicates as one. Test many hooks against a few bodies and a few calls to action, change one
variable per test, and write a hypothesis for each test. A lead magnet solves one narrow problem
completely, in a way that reveals the next problem the offer solves.

## 9. The check {#check}

Read the piece cold, as the brief's reader meeting it in that channel. Each must hold:

1. **Hook:** would they stop for the first line or first second? Does it open on their situation, not
   the product's name or the writer's numbers?
2. **Reader:** is it about them, at their stage of awareness? Internal numbers dressed up as the story
   always fail.
3. **Specificity:** a concrete scene, example or number that makes the point; where the form carries
   pictures, the planned one is there.
4. **One idea:** could they repeat it to a friend in a sentence?
5. **Voice:** a person talking, none of the tells (`voice.md`).
6. **Channel fit:** the form, length and rules of that place (`channels.md`), and nothing a moderator
   would remove.

The floor under all six: every claim has its source; nothing is invented; nothing promised that isn't
true. A self-score does not predict quality. Pieces that scored well on every point have been judged
bad on sight. The person's reaction and the result are what count, so treat this as a gate against
obvious faults, not as proof the piece is good.

## 10. After it goes out {#after} {#ceilings}

- **Before posting, predict** the piece's result as a multiple of the person's normal at 6, 24 and 72
  hours, and compare afterwards.
- **Read start, stay, signal:** nobody stopped means the hook failed; they started and left means the
  payoff came too late; they watched and did nothing means it entertained but wasn't memorable. Write
  three notes per piece: why they started, where they left, which line got a reaction.
- **View ceilings** (one creator's diagnostic): stuck under 1,000 views, the hook is unclear (fix
  subject, stakes, simple, short); under 10,000, the topic doesn't touch a pain or desire, or the first
  visual doesn't match the first words; under 100,000, the angle is obvious. Past that it takes
  controversy, which is usually wrong for a brand. A business can be built without ever passing 100,000.
- **Benchmarks** (creators', unverified): Instagram share rate around 4% is excellent; watch-through
  above 50% (60% on 15 s); X reply rate 1 to 3%; LinkedIn engagement 4%+; newsletter opens 30% normal,
  40% great; YouTube click-through 2 to 10% for most channels (YouTube's own range).
- Write the winning **formula** into the house style, not the piece.

## Never

- Invent a number, quote, testimonial, user or result, or present a hypothetical as a real case.
- Borrow the *comment a keyword and I'll DM you* call to action at scale. Platforms ban automated DMs.
- Use a revenue screenshot, a "study shows" number without its caveats, or anyone's content as your own.
